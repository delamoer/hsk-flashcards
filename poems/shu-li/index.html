Shǔ Lí
The Millet Grows Thick

导读About this poem

A traveller walks through fields of millet where a great capital once stood, and grief overwhelms him — but no one understands. This poem gave Chinese a phrase for mourning a fallen country: “the sorrow of the millet”.

题跋 · 解析Commentary

中文解析

传统的说法是：西周灭亡后，周朝迁都到东边的洛邑；一位官员经过旧都镐京，看到当年的宫殿已经变成一片庄稼地，黍子、稷子长得整整齐齐，他走走停停，心里越来越难过。三章里稷从“苗”到“穗”再到“实”，心情从“摇摇”（心神不定）到“如醉”再到“如噎”（像被东西堵住喉咙），时间在走，悲伤也在加深。最打动人的是中间四句：了解我的人说我心里忧愁，不了解我的人问我在找什么——真正的痛苦常常没人懂。最后他只能问苍天：这是谁造成的？“黍离之悲”后来成了固定说法，专门表示对国家灭亡、繁华不再的哀伤。

In English

According to the traditional reading, after the Western Zhou dynasty fell (771 BC) and the court moved east, an official passed through the old capital and found the palaces gone — in their place, neat rows of millet. He walks on, stops, walks again, and his grief grows. Across the three verses the grain grows from shoots to ears to ripe seed, while his heart goes from “unsettled” to “as if drunk” to “as if choking”: time moves on, and the sorrow deepens with it. The lines that hit hardest are in the middle: those who know me say I’m grieving; those who don’t ask what I’m looking for. Real pain is often something no one else can see. In the end he can only ask the sky: who did this? The poem gave Chinese the phrase 黍离之悲, “the sorrow of the millet”, used ever since for mourning a lost country or vanished glory. Even if you set the history aside, it is a powerful poem about walking alone with a grief nobody understands.

作者与背景 The poet & the moment

The Book of Songs gathers 305 songs from about the 11th to the 6th century BC. The “Airs of the Royal Domain” (王风) come from the area around Luoyang after the Zhou court moved there in 770 BC. The author is unknown; the story of the official at the ruined capital comes from the traditional Mao commentary.

读音注意 Pronunciation notes

黍 shǔ；稷 jì；靡靡 mǐ mǐ（脚步迟缓）；噎 yē；中心 zhōng xīn（心中）。无争议读音。
