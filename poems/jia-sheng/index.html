导读About this poem

An emperor summons a brilliant exiled scholar and talks with him until midnight, leaning in eagerly — only to ask about ghosts and spirits, not about the people. A sharp four-line irony about wasted talent.

题跋 · 解析Commentary

中文解析

贾谊是西汉初年的天才政治家，二十多岁就提出很多治国的主张，却被排挤，贬到长沙。几年后汉文帝把他召回，在宣室接见他。据《史记》记载，文帝问的是鬼神的事，贾谊讲得很好，文帝听得入神，半夜里不自觉地把座席往前移。这首诗前两句先扬：皇帝在宣室访求贤才，召回被贬的大臣，贾谊的才华无人能比。第三句一个“可怜”（可惜）一转，第四句点破：不问百姓的事，只问鬼神。前面越写皇帝重视人才，后面的讽刺就越尖锐，这种写法叫“先扬后抑”。诗人借汉代的故事，讽刺晚唐皇帝迷信求仙、不关心百姓，也抒发了自己怀才不遇的感慨。

In English

Jia Yi (200–168 BC) was a young genius of the early Han dynasty who, in his twenties, proposed bold reforms — and was pushed out of court and sent to faraway Changsha. Years later Emperor Wen called him back and received him in the Xuanshi Hall. According to the historian Sima Qian, the emperor asked about ghosts and spirits; Jia Yi answered so well that the emperor, listening late into the night, kept moving his sitting mat closer without noticing. Li Shangyin tells this story in four lines. The first two praise: the emperor seeks talent, summons the exiled minister, and Jia Yi’s gifts are beyond compare. Line three turns on the word 可怜 (“what a pity”), and line four delivers the blow: he asked not about the common people, but about ghosts and gods. The more the opening builds up the emperor’s respect, the sharper the irony at the end — a technique called “raise first, then lower”. Through a Han story the poet criticises late Tang emperors who chased immortality potions and neglected their people, and also voices his own frustration as a talented man nobody used well.

作者与背景 The poet & the moment

Li Shangyin (c. 813–858) was one of the great poets of the late Tang, famous for dense, beautiful and often mysterious verse. Caught between two rival court factions, he spent most of his life in minor posts. Several late Tang emperors were obsessed with Daoist elixirs; Emperor Wuzong died in 846 after taking them.

全文 · 拼音 Full text with pinyin

宣室求贤访逐臣，

xuān shì qiú xián fǎng zhú chén

Seeking wise men, the emperor called the exile back to his hall;

贾生才调更无伦。

jiǎ shēng cái diào gèng wú lún

and Master Jia’s brilliance had no equal.

可怜夜半虚前席，

kě lián yè bàn xū qián xí

What a pity — at midnight he leaned in closer, for nothing:

不问苍生问鬼神。

bù wèn cāng shēng wèn guǐ shén

he asked not about the people, but about ghosts and gods.
