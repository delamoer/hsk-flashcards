Fá Tán
Chopping Sandalwood

导读About this poem

Woodcutters chop trees by the river and ask a sharp question: the lords never plant or hunt, so how come their barns are full of grain and their yards full of game? A work song with a bite from China’s oldest poetry collection.

题跋 · 解析Commentary

中文解析

这是一首伐木工人边干活边唱的歌。开头“坎坎”是砍树的声音，工人们把砍下的檀木放在河岸边，河水清清、泛着波纹——劳动的场面很有画面感。接着他们问了两个尖锐的问题：你们不种地、不收割，凭什么拿走几百捆粮食？你们不打猎，凭什么院子里挂满了野兽？最后一句“彼君子兮，不素餐兮”是反话：那些老爷们可真是“不白吃饭”啊！表面上夸，实际上讽刺他们白吃饭。这种正话反说的手法叫“反讽”。三章反复唱，只换几个字，每唱一遍，不满就更强一分。今天的成语“尸位素餐”里的“素餐”，意思就是白吃饭、不干事。

In English

This is a work song sung by woodcutters on the job. It starts with the sound of the axe, kǎn kǎn, as they chop sandalwood and stack it on the riverbank, where the water runs clear and rippling — a vivid picture of labour. Then come two pointed questions: you never sow or reap, so how do you take three hundred bundles of grain? You never hunt, so why are badgers hanging in your courtyard? The last line, 彼君子兮，不素餐兮 — “Those fine gentlemen surely don’t eat for nothing!” — is irony: it sounds like praise but means the opposite, that the lords eat without working. Saying the reverse of what you mean in order to mock is called fǎnfěng, irony. As in many Book of Songs pieces, the three verses repeat with only a few words changed, and the anger sharpens with each round. The phrase 素餐 (“eating for nothing”) lives on in the idiom 尸位素餐, used for officials who hold a post and draw a salary without doing any work. The song is often paired with “Big Rat” as an early voice of working people.

作者与背景 The poet & the moment

The Book of Songs gathers 305 songs from about the 11th to the 6th century BC. Like “Big Rat”, this comes from the small state of Wei (魏, in today’s southern Shanxi). The author is unknown; the traditional commentary reads it as a criticism of officials who took pay without merit.

坎坎伐檀兮，寘之河之干兮，

kǎn kǎn fá tán xī, zhì zhī hé zhī gān xī

河水清且涟猗。

hé shuǐ qīng qiě lián yī

bù jià bù sè, hú qǔ hé sān bǎi chán xī
