导读About this poem

A palace lady, forgotten by the emperor, sweeps the hall at dawn and envies the crows: they at least fly past the palace where he lives and carry its sunlight on their wings.

题跋 · 解析Commentary

中文解析

这首诗写一位失宠的宫女。“长信”是汉代的长信宫，传说汉成帝的班婕妤失宠后到这里侍奉太后。诗的开头：天一亮宫殿开门，她就拿着扫帚去打扫，扫完无事可做，只好拿着团扇走来走去。团扇在古诗里常比喻被抛弃的女子——夏天离不开，秋天就被扔到一边。后两句是全诗最妙的地方：她白玉一样美的容颜，还比不上又黑又丑的寒鸦；因为寒鸦还能从昭阳殿（皇帝和宠妃住的地方）飞过来，身上带着那边的阳光。“日影”暗指皇帝的恩宠。用美人和乌鸦作对比，不直接说怨，怨却更深，这种写法叫“含蓄”。诗人借宫女的不幸，也写出有才能却不被重用的人的心情。

In English

This poem is spoken for a palace lady who has lost the emperor’s favour. Changxin was a palace of the Han dynasty; the court lady Ban Jieyu is said to have gone there to serve the empress dowager after the emperor turned to a new favourite. At dawn the palace doors open and she takes her broom to sweep; with nothing else to do, she wanders up and down holding a round silk fan. In Chinese poetry such a fan stands for a woman cast aside — needed all summer, tossed away in autumn. The last two lines are the brilliant part: her jade-fair face is worth less than a cold black crow, because the crow can fly over from the Zhaoyang Palace, where the emperor and his favourite live, still carrying its sunlight on its wings. The “sunlight” is the emperor’s favour. Setting a beautiful woman against an ugly crow, the poem never complains outright, and the grief is all the deeper for it — Chinese critics call this hánxù, “holding back”. Through the palace lady, the poet also speaks for talented people who are passed over.

作者与背景 The poet & the moment

Wang Changling (c. 698–757) was famous for his seven-character quatrains, especially frontier poems and “palace laments” (宫怨) like this one. This is the third of five “Autumn Songs of the Changxin Palace”.

全文 · 拼音 Full text with pinyin

奉帚平明金殿开，

fèng zhǒu píng míng jīn diàn kāi

At dawn the golden hall opens; I take up my broom,

且将团扇共徘徊。

qiě jiāng tuán shàn gòng pái huái

then drift up and down with my round silk fan.

玉颜不及寒鸦色，

yù yán bù jí hán yā sè

My fair face counts for less than a black crow’s wing —

犹带昭阳日影来。

yóu dài zhāo yáng rì yǐng lái

the crows, at least, fly in with sunlight from his palace.
