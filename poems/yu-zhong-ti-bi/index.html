导读About this poem

Written on a cell wall by a reformer waiting to be executed. He hopes his comrades who fled will survive, then faces death laughing at the sky. Its third line is one of the most heroic in Chinese.

题跋 · 解析Commentary

中文解析

1898年，清朝的“戊戌变法”失败，谭嗣同本来有机会逃走，但他决定留下来，说变法需要有人流血。这首诗是他在狱中写在墙上的。前两句用了两个东汉的典故（古代的故事）：张俭被朝廷追捕，逃亡时人们看到门就去投宿，冒险收留他；杜根被太后下令打死，却忍住装死，活了下来，后来重新被重用。谭嗣同用这两个人，希望逃走的同伴能像他们一样得到保护、忍辱活下去，将来继续事业。第三句“我自横刀向天笑”写自己：面对屠刀，仰天大笑，毫不害怕。最后一句“去留肝胆两昆仑”意思是：走的人和留下的人，都是肝胆相照的英雄，都像昆仑山一样高大。（这一句的具体所指，学者有不同理解。）全诗悲壮而豪迈，是中国最有名的绝命诗之一。

In English

In 1898 the Hundred Days’ Reform, an attempt to modernise the Qing government, was crushed. Tan Sitong could have escaped, but he chose to stay, saying that no reform in history had succeeded without someone shedding blood. He wrote this poem on the wall of his prison cell. The first couplet uses two stories from the Eastern Han dynasty. Zhang Jian, hunted by the court, fled from door to door, and families risked their lives to take him in. Du Gen was ordered beaten to death by the empress dowager but played dead, survived, and was later restored to office. By naming them, Tan hopes his comrades who fled will be sheltered like Zhang Jian and endure like Du Gen, living on to carry the cause forward. The third line is about himself: “I lift my blade sideways and laugh at the sky” — facing death without fear. The last line says that those who left and those who stayed are all people of true courage, as great as the Kunlun Mountains. Exactly whom the “two Kunluns” refers to has been debated, but the spirit is clear. The poem is tragic and proud at once, and is among the most famous last poems in Chinese.

作者与背景 The poet & the moment

Tan Sitong (1865–1898) was one of the leaders of the Hundred Days’ Reform. When the Empress Dowager Cixi ended the reform, he refused to flee and was executed with five others — remembered as the “Six Gentlemen of the Hundred Days”. He was 33.

全文 · 拼音 Full text with pinyin

望门投止思张俭，

wàng mén tóu zhǐ sī zhāng jiǎn

I think of Zhang Jian, sheltered at any door he came to,

忍死须臾待杜根。

rěn sǐ xū yú dài dù gēn

and of Du Gen, who held on at death’s edge and lived to return.

我自横刀向天笑，

wǒ zì héng dāo xiàng tiān xiào

As for me, I raise my blade and laugh at the sky:

去留肝胆两昆仑。

qù liú gān dǎn liǎng kūn lún

those who left and those who stayed — both stand brave and tall as Kunlun.
