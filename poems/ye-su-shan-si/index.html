导读About this poem

A temple tower so tall you could pick the stars by hand — and you must whisper, or the people in heaven might hear you. Li Bai at his most playful.

题跋 · 解析Commentary

中文解析

诗人晚上住在山上的一座寺庙里，登上寺里的高楼。第一句说楼很高，有一百尺；第二句更夸张：站在楼上，伸手就能摘到星星。后两句换了一个角度：不敢大声说话，怕吵醒天上的神仙。楼高不高，诗人没有再说，可是“怕惊动天上人”已经让人感觉到：楼顶几乎就挨着天了。这种写法叫“夸张”，加上丰富的想象，正是李白最大的特点。全诗像孩子说话一样天真，读起来有趣。“危楼”的“危”不是“危险”，而是“高”，这是古今意思不同的一个好例子。

In English

The poet spends the night at a temple on a mountain and climbs its tower. The first line says the tower is a hundred feet tall; the second goes further — up there, you could reach out and pick the stars. Then the poem changes angle: he doesn’t dare speak loudly, afraid he might disturb the people living in heaven. He never tells us again how high the tower is, but the fear of waking the immortals says it all: the top almost touches the sky. This is kuāzhāng, exaggeration, combined with bold imagination — exactly what Li Bai is loved for. The whole poem has the innocence of a child talking, which makes it fun to read aloud. One word to watch: 危 in 危楼 does not mean “dangerous” as in modern Chinese, but “high” — a good example of a word whose meaning has changed.

作者与背景 The poet & the moment

Li Bai (701–762), the “Immortal of Poetry”, wandered China for much of his life. The temple is traditionally identified with one in Huangmei, Hubei, but this is uncertain. Some scholars doubt the poem is by Li Bai; it is traditionally attributed to him.

危楼高百尺，

wēi lóu gāo bǎi chǐ

The tower soars a hundred feet —

reach out, and you could pick the stars.

不敢高声语，

bù gǎn gāo shēng yǔ

I dare not raise my voice up here,

恐惊天上人。

kǒng jīng tiān shàng rén

for fear of waking the people in the sky.
