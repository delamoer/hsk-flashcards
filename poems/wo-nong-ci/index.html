导读About this poem

A wife answers her husband’s talk of taking a concubine with a playful, unforgettable image: take a lump of clay, shape a you and a me, smash them together, and mould us again — now there is some of you in me and some of me in you.

中文解析

相传元代大书画家赵孟頫中年时想纳妾，他的妻子管道昇没有吵闹，而是写了这首小词给他，赵孟頫读后很受感动，就打消了这个念头。“侬”是吴地方言里的“你”或“我”，“你侬我侬”就是你和我，今天还用来形容恋人非常亲密。开头说我们的感情太深了，热得像火。接着用一个生活里的比喻：拿一块泥，捏一个你，塑一个我；再把两个泥人一起打碎，加水和在一起，重新捏一个你、塑一个我。这样，我的泥里有你，你的泥里有我，再也分不开了。最后说：活着同盖一条被子，死了同睡一口棺材。语言口语化，像在说话，比喻新奇又亲切，把夫妻“你中有我、我中有你”的深情写得非常形象。

In English

The story goes that the great painter and calligrapher Zhao Mengfu, in middle age, thought about taking a concubine. His wife Guan Daosheng didn’t quarrel; she wrote him this little lyric instead — and he was so moved that he dropped the idea. 侬 is a word from the Wu dialects meaning “you” or “I”, so 你侬我侬 is simply “you and me” — a phrase still used today for a couple who are very close. She begins: our love runs deep, as hot as fire. Then comes a homely image anyone can picture. Take a lump of clay, model a you and model a me. Then smash both figures, add water and knead them together, and model a you and a me all over again. Now there is you in my clay and me in yours, and we can never be separated. She ends with a vow: in life we share one quilt, in death one coffin. The language is close to speech, the image fresh and down-to-earth, and it captures perfectly the idea of a married couple being part of each other. It is said to have worked better than any argument.

作者与背景 The poet & the moment

Guan Daosheng (1262–1319) was a painter, calligrapher and poet, famous for her ink bamboo, and the wife of the artist Zhao Mengfu. The story of how this lyric changed her husband’s mind is traditional and may be a later legend.

全文 · 拼音 Full text with pinyin

你侬我侬，忒煞情多；

nǐ nóng wǒ nóng, tè shà qíng duō

You and me — so much love between us,

情多处，热似火；

qíng duō chù, rè sì huǒ

so much it burns like fire.

把一块泥，捻一个你，塑一个我。

bǎ yī kuài ní, niǎn yī gè nǐ, sù yī gè wǒ

Take a lump of clay: shape a you, shape a me.

将咱两个一齐打破，用水调和；

jiāng zán liǎng gè yī qí dǎ pò, yòng shuǐ tiáo hé

Then break the two of us together and knead us with water,

再捻一个你，再塑一个我。

zài niǎn yī gè nǐ, zài sù yī gè wǒ

and shape a you again, and shape a me.

我泥中有你，你泥中有我；

wǒ ní zhōng yǒu nǐ, nǐ ní zhōng yǒu wǒ

Now there is you in my clay, and me in yours.

我与你生同一个衾，死同一个椁。

wǒ yǔ nǐ shēng tóng yī gè qīn, sǐ tóng yī gè guǒ

Living, we’ll share one quilt; dead, we’ll share one grave.
